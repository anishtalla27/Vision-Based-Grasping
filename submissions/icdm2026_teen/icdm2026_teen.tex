\begin{abstract}
I compared three ways to predict a robotic grasp from an RGB image: a
detector-assisted heuristic, a ResNet18 trained on grasp labels, and GPT-4o,
a general-purpose vision-language model (VLM), used zero-shot. The protocol
fixes the test images, input, output representation, and scoring rule on an
object-wise split of the Cornell Grasping Dataset. It does not equalize
supervision or tuning, so it compares complete pipelines. ResNet18 passed on
79.7\% of test images, and a second, three-seed round reached 84.0\% with
ResNet34. The
heuristic passed on 40.7\% as frozen and on 57.7\% after a fix prompted by
test output; every statistical comparison uses the clean 40.7\%. GPT-4o
passed on 12.4\% of calls across five independent runs per image. Under
object-clustered resampling, ResNet18 led the heuristic by 39.0 points and
the heuristic led one-call GPT-4o by 28.3. ResNet18 missed mostly on
placement, while GPT-4o failed both criteria on 59.3\% of
calls and put its predicted center outside the hull of all labeled grasp
corners on 53.4\%. That pattern suggested GPT-4o might see a good grasp but
fail to write it as coordinates. A fourth run tested this by showing the same
model label-free candidate grasps drawn on the image and asking it to choose
one by number. On three menus, one with every mark drawn the same size, its
accuracy stayed at the menu's random floor, and it preferred closing along
the object's long axis. Removing the coordinate output did not help, so the
main failure is in choosing the grasp.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A robot has to make a geometric decision before it can close its gripper:
where the fingers should land, how the gripper should rotate, and how far the
jaws should open. Someone building a first grasp predictor has three
practical starting points. They can write the decision as rules, train a
network on labeled grasps, or ask a hosted VLM, which needs no training data
and almost no setup. The costs differ sharply, but published numbers do not
say which to pick, because each family is usually tested in its own way.
GR-ConvNet reports 96.6\% object-wise on an extended Cornell set with
RGB-D input and a prediction at every pixel \cite{kumra2020}.
Single-rectangle regression reported 84.9\% with RGB-D \cite{redmon2015}. VLM grasping systems such as
VLAD-Grasp are scored as whole pipelines in which the VLM is one stage among
depth prediction, segmentation, and pose recovery \cite{kulshrestha2025}.
Rule-based baselines are often described rather than scored. When input,
output, split, and metric all change with the method, the gap between two
papers mixes the method with the test.

I held those four things fixed and changed only where the grasp decision
comes from. Every system saw the same 123 held-out RGB images of 35 objects,
returned one grasp rectangle, and was scored by one implementation of the
standard rectangle test \cite{jiang2011,lenz2015}. I reconstructed object
identities so that no physical object crosses between training and test.
``Unified'' here means the same input, output, and metric. It does not mean
equal footing: the systems differ in supervision, scaffolding, and
development effort, and Table~\ref{tab:inputs} lists those differences
instead of hiding them. Each system is a complete pipeline of the kind a
practitioner would build, so the results compare pipelines, not isolated
reasoning abilities.

The paper asks two questions. First, under a fixed test, how much does the
source of grasp knowledge (hand-written rules, task labels, or web-scale
pretraining) change accuracy and where the errors fall? Second, when the VLM
fails, is the failure in choosing a grasp or in writing it as pixel
coordinates? Prior VLM pipelines sidestep the second question by design, since
they rarely ask the model for final coordinates
\cite{yang2024,kulshrestha2025}. I test it directly with a fourth run that
keeps the model and images and removes the coordinate output.

The contribution is a measurement, not a new grasping method: a
same-protocol comparison of three families on an object-wise split, with
object-clustered statistics and the clean and post-hoc results kept apart; a
shared failure taxonomy and a center-hull check that locate where each family
fails; and a direct test of the most natural explanation for the VLM's
failure, which that explanation does not survive.

\section{Related Work}

Jiang \emph{et al.} introduced the Cornell dataset and its rectangle
representation of a grasp, which stores the center, orientation, opening,
and jaw depth \cite{jiang2011}. Under the usual Cornell test, a prediction
passes when it is within $30^\circ$ of a labeled grasp and its intersection
over union (IoU) is greater than 25\% \cite{lenz2015}. Redmon and Angelova
regressed one rectangle per image from RGB-D and reported 84.9\%
object-wise \cite{redmon2015}, the published design closest to System B.
GR-ConvNet's dense pixel-wise output reaches higher scores \cite{kumra2020}.

VLM-based grasping systems usually do not ask the language model for the
final pixel coordinates. Set-of-Mark prompting has the model choose among
regions already marked on an image \cite{yang2024}, and System D borrows
that idea. VLAD-Grasp has a VLM draw the grasp, then recovers a pose with
depth prediction, segmentation, and point-cloud alignment
\cite{kulshrestha2025}. System C tests a smaller pipeline: show GPT-4o the
image and ask it to return the coordinates itself. Its score describes that
output method, not VLM-based grasping in general.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Dataset, Split, and Metric}

Cornell contains 885 RGB-D images of household objects, with several positive
grasp rectangles on most images. I used only RGB. Cornell does not include
object IDs, so I reconstructed them from the frame sequence. The procedure
segmented the object from the photography platform and compared consecutive
masks with color and area descriptors that tolerate rotation. I set the
thresholds asymmetrically because the two possible mistakes have different
costs. If two objects are merged, the combined group still stays in one
split. If one object is split in two, its images can leak into both training
and test.

Of the 884 frame boundaries, 251 (28.4\%) fell in the ambiguous band. An
automated first pass gave each one a decision and a confidence. In a blind
30-boundary audit, its calls agreed with my labels on 86.7\% of confident
cases but only 40.0\% of uncertain ones, so its uncertain calls were not
used. I decided 139 boundaries by hand, covering every uncertain call, every
high-risk ``different object'' call, and every call whose reasoning used a
flagged error pattern, and accepted the other 112
confident same-object calls, where an error can only merge groups. Six
boundaries stayed ambiguous after review. Four leaned toward ``same object''
and were merged. The other two had no lean, so instead of guessing I dropped
the one image on the smaller side of each (pcd0435 and pcd0782). The open
question in both cases was object identity, not the grasp annotations. That
left 883 images in 234 groups, slightly under the 240 objects reported for this dataset
\cite{redmon2015}, as expected from thresholds that err toward merging. With
seed 42, training holds 164 objects (620 images), validation 35 (140), and
test 35 (123). A second check confirmed that no object appears in more than
one partition.

I scored each prediction against every positive rectangle on its image. A
match to any one of them counts as correct. Before using the evaluator at
scale, I checked hand-computed cases, rectangle round trips, and an independent
rasterized IoU calculation. All predictions were converted back to the native
$640\times480$ coordinate frame for the final comparison.

\subsection{System A: Detector-Assisted Heuristic}
\label{sec:systema}

System A starts with a COCO-pretrained Faster R-CNN using a
ResNet50-FPN-v2 backbone \cite{ren2015}. A fixed lookup table maps the detected
category to a center, upper, or end grasp. I calibrated three global
opening, jaw-size, and offset constants on training labels only. The COCO
vocabulary misses most Cornell objects, so a platform-segmentation fallback
supplies a box when the detector cannot. The rule closes the gripper across
the box's narrow dimension. It can output only $0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$.

The frozen table scored 40.7\% on its first and only clean test run. Its
rendered predictions then exposed a defect: some accepted detections covered
background clutter instead of the object on the platform. I added a guard
requiring the detection to contain the segmented object's centroid and
recalibrated the constants on training data, and the score rose to 57.7\%.
Test output prompted that change, so 40.7\% is System A's result and is the
value used in every statistical comparison. The 57.7\% is reported only as a
marked post-hoc figure.

\subsection{System B: Learned Predictor}

System B replaces ResNet18's ImageNet classifier \cite{he2016} with separate
heads for center, orientation, and size. The orientation head predicts
$(\cos 2\theta,\sin 2\theta)$. Doubling the angle handles the fact that a
parallel-jaw grasp is unchanged by a $180^\circ$ rotation. For images with
several labels, I compute the loss against every rectangle and backpropagate
only the best match. Averaging all valid labels could put the target between
two good grasps, where neither one actually exists.

I trained a custom CNN, ResNet18, and ResNet34 on the 620 training images.
Affine augmentation moved the rectangle corners with the pixels. Architecture,
checkpoint, size-loss weight, and the custom CNN's learning rate were chosen
on validation data. ResNet18 had the best validation accuracy at 83.6\%, so I
froze that model before opening the test results.

That first round trained each architecture once, at one seed, with a
constant learning rate, and kept the best of roughly a hundred noisy
validation epochs. A second round kept the model, loss, data, and split and
changed only the recipe: cosine learning-rate decay over 100 epochs, an
exponential moving average of the weights, three seeds per architecture, and
no checkpoint selection. Test-time augmentation over eight dihedral views,
combined by taking the medoid rectangle, was adopted only because it raised
mean validation accuracy. Four selection rules were written down before the
test split was opened a second time, and every configuration that touched
test is reported.

\subsection{System C: Zero-Shot VLM}

System C sends the native RGB image to GPT-4o through OpenRouter
\cite{openai2024}. It uses no grasp-specific training. The prompt asks for two
fingertip coordinates and a jaw width in JSON, which together define the
rectangle. I developed the prompt on 30 training images and froze it before
test.

Each test image was sent in five independent, single-turn calls at the
provider's default temperature. I retried transport failures, but not replies
that arrived and failed to parse. Retrying those would quietly turn a
single-call baseline into a best-of-several system. Of 615 calls, 614 parsed.
The primary score pools the five exchangeable runs to estimate one-call
accuracy. Best-of-five is only a ceiling: it requires five calls and an oracle
that knows when one of them is right.

\subsection{System D: Marked-Candidate Selection}

If GPT-4o can see where to grasp but cannot express it as coordinates,
removing the coordinate output should recover its choice. System D shows the
same model the same test images with candidate grasps drawn on a zoomed crop
and asks for the number of the one it would use. Candidates come only from
the platform-segmentation mask: principal-component analysis gives the
centroid, long axis, and extent, and candidates are positions along the long
axis crossed with four jaw-travel directions 45$^\circ$ apart, so every grasp
angle is within the metric's tolerance of some candidate. Opening and jaw
width use System A's train-calibrated constants. Numbering is re-shuffled on
every call. Because the menu is fixed before the model sees it, each run
carries its own floor (expected accuracy of a uniformly random choice) and
ceiling (whether any candidate passes).

Three menus were run, each on the full test split with five repeats: twelve
marks at three positions; four marks at the centroid only, drawn at real
size; and the same four marks drawn at one uniform length, with one added
prompt sentence stating that length is not to scale. Eleven test images have
no mask and so no candidates; they count as misses in accuracy, floor, and
ceiling, leaving 560 calls per menu. The last two exist because on thin
objects the correct marks are the smallest on the image, so a preference for
large marks would mimic a preference for the long-axis grasp. The prompts were
developed on 30 training images for parse rate and glyph reading only, and
each menu's test run was called once under its own sentinel.

\subsection{Statistical and Failure Analysis}

The 123 test images are repeated views of 35 unseen objects, not 123
independent samples. I formed the 95\% confidence intervals from 20,000
object-clustered bootstrap resamples. Every view of an object, along with all
five VLM calls, moves together. Paired differences use the same resamples and
20,000 object-level label-swap permutations.

The failure analysis gives each attempt one label: correct, angle only,
overlap only, both criteria, or no prediction. I also checked whether the
predicted center falls inside the convex hull of all labeled grasp corners on
the image. This is a loose spatial check, not a claim that the grasp would
work on hardware.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Overall Accuracy}

ResNet18 passed 98 of 123 images (79.7\%). The heuristic passed 50 as frozen
(40.7\%) and 71 after the post-hoc fix (57.7\%). GPT-4o passed 76 of 615
calls (12.4\%), and its five runs ranged from 9.8\% to 14.6\%. At least one
call passed on 43 images, a best-of-five ceiling of 35.0\%.
Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} gives the object-clustered intervals.

Every paired test uses the clean heuristic. ResNet18 led it by 39.0 points
[25.2, 52.9], with permutation $p<0.0001$, and the heuristic led one-call
GPT-4o by 28.3 points [16.9, 40.0], $p=0.0001$. The heuristic's lead over
GPT-4o's best-of-five ceiling, 5.7 points [$-6.1$, 18.0], $p=0.45$, does
not exclude zero. That comparison favored the heuristic by 22.8 points only
after the post-hoc fix, so it is not a supported result. Giving each object
equal weight produced 38.5\%, 75.4\%, and 10.5\% for A, B, and one-call C,
which changed the gaps but not their order.

The second training round chose ResNet34 on mean final-weight validation
accuracy (81.4\% against 81.2\% for ResNet18). Its three seeds scored 84.6,
83.7, and 83.7\% on test; ResNet18 under the same recipe averaged 82.6\%.
Re-running the first-round recipe at two new seeds gave 82.9\% and 80.5\%,
so 79.7\% was a low-side single draw, and the first round's nine-point gap
between ResNet18 and ResNet34 was run-to-run noise. Round 2 leads the clean
heuristic by 43.4 points [29.9, 56.7], $p<0.0001$; its 4.3-point lead over
round 1 does not exclude zero. Splits and training data differ from
\cite{redmon2015}, but System B's RGB-only score is within 5.2 points of that
RGB-D single-rectangle result in round 1, and 0.9 in round 2.

\subsection{Failure Patterns}

The error counts in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} separate the systems more
clearly than accuracy alone. The frozen heuristic most often failed both
tests at once (34 of its 73 failures), and its predicted center fell outside
the labeled-grasp hull on 28 of 115 predictions (24.3\%), the mark of the
clutter boxes the fix later rejected. After the fix, 13 of A's 52 failures
missed only the angle test. That is partly built in, since A can output only
$0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$, but the limit is loose: only 5 test images (4.1\%)
have no labeled grasp within the metric's $30^\circ$ of either axis, and 103
(83.7\%) have one within $15^\circ$. On the 20 images whose labeled grasps
all lie more than $15^\circ$ from both axes, post-hoc A passed 40.0\%,
against 61.2\% on the rest. B had four angle-only failures; 15 of its 25
misses failed only on overlap, so placement was its larger problem. C failed
both tests on 365 of 615 calls (59.3\%).

\subsection{System D}

Table~\ref{tab:menus} gives the three menus. On none of them does the
model's one-call accuracy differ from a random choice among the same
candidates (paired object-clustered differences $-1.4$ [$-6.6$, $+4.0$],
$+1.9$ [$-2.2$, $+6.3$], and $-2.3$ [$-6.3$, $+1.7$]), while the ceilings
are 79.7\% and 65.9\%. Its choices are not random, though. On the full
menu it chose the candidate whose jaws travel along the object's long axis
near one end in 61\% of calls, a grasp type that passes 17\% of the time,
with reasoning such as ``grips the narrow top edge near the center of
mass''. With four marks at the centroid drawn at one uniform size, it still
chose the long-axis direction in 45\% of calls against a 25\% chance rate,
and the short-axis candidate, which passes on 55\% of images, in 17\%.
Stated confidence was ``high'' on 556 of 560 full-menu calls, 21\% of them
correct. Every reply parsed. The centroid candidate closing across the short
axis, with no model at all, scored 55.3\% [41.8, 68.0], level with the
corrected heuristic. It was defined after the main runs and reuses A's
post-fix constants, so it belongs with the post-hoc figures.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why GPT-4o Fails}

Before System D, three explanations fit C's results. The model might pick a
sensible region in words and lose it while writing pixels (coordinate
binding); it might misread the image's coordinate frame or scale; or it might
choose a poor grasp in the first place. The informal evidence for coordinate
binding was that the rationales I read on a sample of C's failures named a
plausible part of the object while the coordinates landed elsewhere, which
was not a blinded study. The first two explanations both blame the
coordinate output, so both predict that removing it would move accuracy
toward the menu's 79.7\% ceiling. System D removed coordinates and scale
altogether, and accuracy stayed at the random floor on all three menus. The
preference for closing along the long axis survived a menu with no
end-straddling candidates and one with every mark the same size. Coordinate
errors may still add to C's misses (D minus C was $+6.3$ [$-1.6$, $+15.0$],
$p=0.19$), but they cannot account for them. From a top-down photograph, two
fingertip pads straddling the end of an elongated object do look like they
pinch a narrow part; in three dimensions the second finger lands on top of
the object. The evidence fits a failure of grasp reasoning from a single 2-D
view better than a failure to bind text to coordinates.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

For someone choosing a starting point, three conclusions follow on this
benchmark. With a few hundred labeled images, fine-tuning a small pretrained
CNN was the strongest option by a wide margin, and its remaining errors were
placement errors, which points to where more data or a dense output would
help. Without labels, the object mask carried most of the useful signal: the
COCO detector missed most Cornell objects and, as frozen, often boxed
background clutter, while the label-free short-axis rule, a post-hoc
baseline, scored 55.3\%.
Zero-shot GPT-4o should not be the source of grasp geometry, whether asked
for coordinates or for a choice among marks. Its stated confidence, ``high'' on
556 of 560 full-menu calls, cannot filter bad grasps, and repeated calls on
one image agreed on only 22\% of rectangles. A VLM in a grasping system
should leave the geometric decision
to another component, as VLAD-Grasp does with depth and point-cloud
alignment \cite{kulshrestha2025}. For evaluation practice, the 17-point move
from one test-prompted fix shows why the clean number should carry the
inference.

\subsection{Limitations}

The evaluation is narrow: one object-wise split of one small benchmark, RGB
without depth, one VLM with one prompt per output format, and no robot
trials. The 123 test images come from only 35 objects, so the intervals are
wide, and a fresh split, object-wise cross-validation, or a second dataset is
needed to confirm the ranking. Cornell's annotations are not exhaustive, so a
rectangle that fails the metric might still work on a real object. The
development budgets differ (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}). B received three
architectures, validation tuning, and a second training round, against one
frozen prompt for C and two near-identical prompts for D. The test split was opened twice
for B under rules fixed beforehand, and twice for A, whose second result is
post-hoc. System D used one glyph style and one candidate generator. An early
formatting check on about 550 grasp rectangles probably included a few images
that later entered test, although no parameter or rule came from that check.

\section{Conclusion}

Under one fixed input, output, and metric, ResNet18 scored 79.7\% (ResNet34
reached 84.0\% over three seeds in a second round), the frozen heuristic 40.7\% (57.7\%
after a post-hoc fix), and GPT-4o 12.4\% per call. Their error locations add
context to that ranking. The frozen heuristic often acted on background
clutter, ResNet18 usually got the angle and missed the placement, and GPT-4o
often failed before either distinction mattered. Given marked candidate
grasps instead of a coordinate output, the same model chose no better than
chance on three menus and kept preferring a grasp along the object's long
axis even with every mark drawn the same size. On this benchmark the problem
is the grasp choice, not only its expression. Whether depth input, or a
model with a stronger three-dimensional prior, changes that choice is the
next question.
